XRD

X-ray diffraction: principles and material examples

Principle

X-rays scatter from the atoms in a material. In a crystal, regularly spaced atomic planes produce constructive interference when Bragg’s law, nλ = 2d sinθ, is satisfied. The measured peak position is 2θ; half that angle is used to calculate the interplanar spacing d.

OutcomeIdentify crystalline phases and follow lattice-spacing changes. With suitable models and corrections, peak profiles and integrated scattering can also inform coherent domain size, strain, texture and crystallinity. The guide compares glasses, polymers, single crystals, phosphors, ceramics, metals, nanomaterials and glass-ceramics.

Principle reference: Yale · XRD principle

The big idea

Every solid is made of atoms. In some materials the atoms sit in neat, repeating rows, like soldiers on a parade ground. In others they are jumbled, like a crowd. X-ray diffraction (XRD) tells you which of these a material is, and, if it is ordered, which arrangement it has.

X-rays are used because their wavelength (about 0.154 nm for the common copper source) is close to the gap between neighbouring atoms. Waves bend and interfere when they meet objects about their own size, so rows of atoms act on X-rays like a very fine grating.

  • Is it ordered? A crystal has long-range periodic order; a glass lacks it but retains local order.
  • Which material is it? Each crystal structure gives its own fingerprint.
  • How large are the coherent domains, and how strained? Peak analysis can probe crystallite size, strain and texture; suitable methods estimate crystallinity.

Why peaks appear: Bragg's law

Imagine the atoms as balls on stacked shelves. Each shelf bounces a little of the X-ray beam. The ray that reaches the second shelf travels a bit further than the one bounced from the first (the amber stretch in Figure 1). When that extra distance is exactly one wavelength, or two, or three, the bounced waves line up and reinforce; the detector sees a strong signal. At other angles the waves cancel.

nλ = 2 d sinθ

Here λ is the X-ray wavelength, d is the spacing between atomic planes, θ is the Bragg angle between the incident beam and those planes, and n is an integer. The plotted diffraction angle is 2θ, so halve a measured peak position before using the equation. For first order (n = 1), d = λ ÷ (2 sinθ).

The instrument

A diffractometer records diffracted intensity versus the angle 2θ between the incident and diffracted beams. The moving components depend on the geometry: in θ–θ instruments the tube and detector move around a stationary sample; in θ–2θ instruments the source can remain fixed while the sample and detector rotate. Powders, films, solid pieces and wafers need appropriate mounting and optics. XRD is often nondestructive, although beam-sensitive materials need care.

Three kinds of order, three pictures

  • Single crystal: a common lattice orientation gives discrete diffraction spots. The reflections captured depend on crystal orientation and measurement geometry.
  • Powder or polycrystal: many randomly oriented crystallites produce rings. Texture or limited sampling can instead give arcs or spotty rings.
  • Glass or amorphous: lack of long-range periodicity produces broad diffuse scattering, sometimes with more than one halo.

Azimuthal integration of an area-detector image gives intensity versus 2θ. Powder rings yield sharp peaks and amorphous scattering yields broad features. A single-crystal image samples only the reflections accessible in that orientation; it is not a complete powder pattern.

Reading a pattern: four clues

ClueWhat to look atWhat it tells you
1 PositionWhere the peaks sit (angle 2θ)Spacing between atomic planes, hence the size of the unit cell and which phase it is
2 HeightHow tall each peak isWhich atoms sit where, and how the grains are oriented
3 WidthHow broad each peak isCoherent domain size and lattice strain, after instrumental correction
4 HumpA broad swell under or between peaksThe disordered part: glass, tangled polymer chains, amorphous phases
A worked example: peaks that move

For first-order diffraction with λ = 1.54 Å, a peak at 2θ = 9.5° gives d ≈ 9.3 Å; at 2θ = 6.5°, d ≈ 13.6 Å. For the same indexed reflection, a shift to lower 2θ means a larger interplanar spacing. Intercalation or lattice expansion can cause this, but alignment, sample displacement and changes of phase or indexing must also be checked.

What XRD shows for each kind of material

All patterns in this document were simulated for teaching, using standard lattice sizes and peak positions where applicable. Peak heights and widths are illustrative.

Glass (amorphous)

A glass lacks long-range periodic order but retains short- and intermediate-range correlations. It therefore shows broad diffuse features rather than a set of sharp Bragg peaks. Silica glass often has a broad feature near 22° with Cu Kα radiation. That feature is not a direct measurement of a nearest-neighbour bond length; total-scattering pair-distribution-function analysis is needed for real-space distances.

  • What you see: broad diffuse scattering, with no resolved crystalline peaks in the illustrated scan.
  • What it means: local atomic order remains, while long-range lattice periodicity is absent.
  • What you use it for: assessing predominantly amorphous character. No visible peaks does not exclude crystalline material below the detection limit or very small crystallites.
Polymers

Polymers may be amorphous or semicrystalline. In a semicrystalline polymer, ordered chain regions give diffraction peaks on top of diffuse scattering from disordered regions. An area method estimates Xc (%) = Ac / At × 100, where Ac is the integrated crystalline scattering and At = Ac + Aa includes crystalline and amorphous scattering after background subtraction. Peak separation and baseline choice affect the result; it is a method-dependent estimate rather than a universal mass fraction. The simulated example illustrates about 35% crystallinity.

  • What you see: sharp peaks on a broad hump.
  • What you use it for: estimating percentage crystallinity and following how processing or ageing changes it.
Single crystals

A symmetric θ–2θ scan of a single-crystal wafer selects lattice planes parallel to the surface. Cubic Si (100), equivalently indexed as Si (001), can show the allowed {400} reflection near 69.1° with Cu Kα radiation. Other geometries access other reflections. Peak position and width can inform strain and structural quality, but instrumental resolution, rocking curves and reciprocal-space maps must be considered.

  • What you see: one or a few very sharp peaks (or spots).
  • What you use it for: checking orientation, quality and strain of crystals and wafers.
Phosphors

Phosphors often contain a crystalline host with a small amount of luminescent dopant. XRD checks the host structure and looks for detectable secondary phases. Substitution can change lattice parameters and shift peaks, but a small shift alone does not prove dopant incorporation. Reference matching, calibrated peak positions and complementary composition measurements strengthen the assignment.

  • What you see: many sharp peaks that can be compared with a reference card.
  • What you use it for: phase purity and tracking how doping changes the lattice.
Ceramics

Crystalline ceramic grains produce characteristic diffraction peaks. Comparing a full pattern with reference data helps identify the phases present. Additional peaks may reveal secondary phases, subject to overlap and detection limits. Quantitative phase fractions require an appropriate whole-pattern or calibrated method; peak heights alone are insufficient.

  • What you see: sharp peaks at fixed positions.
  • What you use it for: identifying phases, spotting impurities and measuring lattice size precisely.
Metals

Metals are polycrystals, so their pattern looks like a powder pattern. Copper has a face-centred cubic lattice with peaks near 43.3°, 50.4° and 74.1°. If the grains are lined up, for example by rolling a sheet, the relative peak heights change (here the (200) peak grows and (111) shrinks). Processing strain shifts and broadens peaks.

  • What you see: a few sharp peaks whose positions are set by the lattice.
  • What you use it for: lattice size, grain orientation (texture) and strain from rolling or heat treatment.
Nanomaterials

A smaller coherent diffracting domain gives broader diffraction peaks. The Scherrer equation estimates the domain size perpendicular to the diffracting planes when size broadening can be separated from instrumental and strain effects:

D = K λ ÷ (β cosθ), with K ≈ 0.9 for this example

Here β is the sample size-broadening FWHM in radians of 2θ, after instrumental correction, and θ is half the peak position 2θ. At the Au (111) peak near 38.2° with λ = 1.54 Å and K = 0.9, ideal size-only FWHM values are about 0.17°, 0.84° and 2.8° for domains of 50, 10 and 3 nm. The plotted curves are illustrative and may include additional width. Crystallite size is not necessarily particle size; strain, defects and instrumental resolution can also broaden peaks.

  • What you see: the same peak positions as the bulk material, but much broader.
  • What you use it for: estimating coherent domain size and following growth, with suitable instrumental and strain corrections.
Glass-ceramics

A glass-ceramic contains crystals within a residual glassy matrix. Its pattern can combine Bragg peaks with diffuse scattering. Changes during heat treatment track crystallisation and phase formation. Quantitative crystal-to-glass fractions require suitable calibration or a quantitative refinement method; peak height or hump size alone is insufficient. The illustrated peak positions represent a lithium-disilicate-type phase.

  • What you see: peaks sitting on a leftover hump.
  • What you use it for: following crystallisation and checking which crystals formed.

What XRD cannot tell you easily

  • Small amounts can be missed: the detection limit depends on phase, matrix, crystallinity, peak overlap, instrument and acquisition conditions; there is no single universal percentage.
  • Light atoms: hydrogen, lithium and similar atoms scatter X-rays very weakly.
  • Preferred orientation: plate-like or needle-like grains can distort peak heights.
  • Sample height error: a misplaced surface shifts every peak.
  • Overlapping peaks: different phases or broad peaks can mask each other.
  • The illuminated volume is averaged: a conventional laboratory scan reports contributions from the sampled material rather than one selected particle. For glasses and liquids, total-scattering pair-distribution-function analysis, NMR or Raman can add structural information.

Takeaways

  • Bragg's law links peak angle to the spacing between atomic planes.
  • Sharp peaks mean order; a broad hump means disorder.
  • Broader peaks can indicate smaller coherent domains, but strain and instrumental broadening must also be evaluated.
  • Peak positions fingerprint the crystal phase.

FTIR

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy

Principle

Broadband infrared light passes through an interferometer, where a moving mirror produces a changing optical-path difference. The detector records an interferogram after interaction with the sample; a Fourier transform converts it into an infrared spectrum. Absorption occurs when the light couples to a vibration that changes the molecular dipole moment.

OutcomeFunctional groups and bonding in ligands, polymers, and inorganic networks. Comparing bands before and after treatment helps follow surface chemistry, while overlapping bands require cautious assignments.

Principle reference: Thermo Fisher · FTIR

Raman

Raman spectroscopy

Principle

A laser illuminates the sample, and most scattered photons retain the laser’s energy. A small fraction exchange energy with lattice or molecular vibrations and emerge at shifted frequencies. Filters reject the strong elastic scattering, while a spectrometer measures the Raman shift relative to the excitation laser.

OutcomeVibrational fingerprints of phases, bonds, and structural changes. Peak shifts and widths can reflect strain or disorder, although heating and fluorescence backgrounds must be checked.

Principle reference: Thermo Fisher · Raman
04

Absorption

UV–vis–NIR absorption spectroscopy

Principle

A spectrophotometer compares the light transmitted through a sample with a suitable blank or reference at each wavelength. Absorbance is A = log₁₀(I₀/I), where I₀ and I are the incident-reference and transmitted intensities. Peaks and edges show which photon energies the material absorbs; scattering can also reduce transmission and should be distinguished from true absorption.

OutcomeElectronic transitions, excitonic features, and optical absorption edges. Concentration can be obtained with a suitable Beer–Lambert calibration; extracting a band gap requires an appropriate optical model.

Principle reference: Shimadzu · Spectrophotometry
05

Emission / PL

Steady-state photoluminescence

Principle

Light at a selected excitation wavelength is absorbed and populates excited electronic states. After relaxation, a fraction of the energy returns as emitted photons; a spectrometer separates those photons by wavelength and a detector records their intensity. In a solution measurement, collection at 90° to the excitation beam helps reduce directly transmitted light, as illustrated here.

OutcomeEmission color, peak position, linewidth, dopant transitions, and changes in luminescence after passivation or doping. Intensity comparisons require matched excitation, concentration, and collection conditions; PL intensity alone is not quantum yield.

06

Lifetime / TRPL

Time-resolved photoluminescence

Principle

A short optical pulse creates an excited population, and the detector measures how the photoluminescence changes with time after the pulse. The decay is fitted using a suitable model while accounting for the instrument response. For a simple single population, τ = 1/(kᵣ + kₙᵣ): both radiative emission and nonradiative loss shorten the lifetime.

OutcomeRecombination, trapping, and energy-transfer dynamics. Combining lifetime with quantum yield can help distinguish changes in radiative and nonradiative rates; a longer lifetime alone does not establish better emission efficiency.

Principle reference: HORIBA · Lifetime methods

Ultrafast transient absorption

Time-resolved pump–probe spectroscopy

Principle

Transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy, also called pump–probe spectroscopy, watches how the absorption of a sample changes after it has been excited by light. A short, strong pulse (the pump) prepares excited states. A second, weak pulse (the probe) arrives a controlled time later and measures what the sample now absorbs. Repeating the measurement at many delays turns a series of snapshots into a movie of the excited-state dynamics.

OutcomeFollow excited-state populations and the rates of carrier cooling, energy transfer, trapping, and recombination. Read wavelength-resolved spectra and kinetic traces together to separate overlapping bleach, emission, and induced-absorption signals.

The idea

Transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy, also called pump–probe spectroscopy, watches how the absorption of a sample changes after it has been excited by light. A short, strong pulse (the pump) prepares excited states. A second, weak pulse (the probe) arrives a controlled time later and measures what the sample now absorbs. Repeating the measurement at many delays turns a series of snapshots into a movie of the excited-state dynamics.

The time resolution comes from the pulse width, typically about 100 fs, because the delay is set by the path length of light rather than by electronics. This is why TA can follow processes that no detector could resolve directly.

The experiment

A femtosecond laser is split into two beams. The pump beam passes through an optical parametric amplifier (OPA) or a harmonic generator so that its wavelength can be matched to an absorption band of the sample, and a chopper blocks every other pulse. The probe beam travels through a motorized delay stage and then through a crystal that converts it into a white-light continuum. After passing through the sample, the probe is dispersed onto an array detector.

Role of the pump
  • Strong and tunable; its wavelength is chosen so that the sample absorbs it.
  • Creates the excited states: excitons, free carriers, radicals or other species.
  • Its fluence (energy per area) sets how many excitations are formed, and should usually stay in the linear regime.
Role of the probe
  • Weak, so that it measures the sample without creating excitations of its own.
  • Broadband (white light), so a whole spectrum is recorded at each delay.
  • Delayed by Δt relative to the pump; the measurement is made with and without the pump.

The delay Δt

What the delay is. Δt is the time by which the probe arrives after the pump. At Δt = 0 the two pulses overlap. At Δt < 0 the probe arrives before the pump and records the unperturbed sample, which is a useful reference. At Δt > 0 the probe measures the sample as it evolves.

How it is set. The probe passes over a mirror on a motorized stage. Light travels about 0.3 µm per femtosecond and the path is traversed twice, so moving the mirror by 1 µm changes the delay by about 6.7 fs. A stage of 30 cm gives roughly 2 ns of delay. Longer delays (ns to µs) need electronic delay between two synchronized lasers.

What is measured

The quantity recorded is the pump-induced change in absorbance:

ΔA(λ, Δt) = A(pump on) − A(pump off) = −log₁₀(I_on / I_off)

A negative ΔA means the sample absorbs less light with the pump on; a positive ΔA means it absorbs more. (Some groups plot ΔT/T instead, which reverses the sign of the bleach.)

The signals: bleach and the others

SignalSignOriginSpectral signature
Ground-state bleach (GSB)ΔA < 0The pump removes molecules from S₀, so fewer are available to absorb the probe.Mirrors the steady-state absorption band
Stimulated emission (SE)ΔA < 0The probe stimulates S₁ → S₀ emission, adding photons to the probe beam.Mirrors the fluorescence, shifted to longer wavelength
Excited-state absorption (ESA)ΔA > 0The excited state absorbs the probe and moves to a higher state (S₁ → Sₙ).Broad, no counterpart in the steady-state spectrum
Product / photoinduced absorption (PIA)ΔA > 0A new species (triplet, charge-separated state, trapped carrier) absorbs.Rises as the species forms

Because the signals overlap, a net spectrum can hide one of them. Measuring the steady-state absorption and photoluminescence first tells you where the bleach and stimulated emission should appear; what remains is excited-state or product absorption.

Kinetics and the full data set

  • Bleach recovery tracks how fast the ground state refills.
  • ESA decay tracks loss of the excited state.
  • PIA rise shows when a new species forms.
  • Matching rates in different spectral regions suggest the same species.

Global analysis fits all wavelengths and delays together with a sum of exponentials, giving lifetimes and the spectrum associated with each component (decay-associated or evolution-associated spectra).

Semiconductors and nanocrystals

In semiconductors and nanocrystals (for example CsPbBr₃) the band-edge bleach is often proportional to the number of carriers, because occupied states block the probe transition. Keep the pump fluence low enough to avoid multi-carrier effects and Auger recombination unless these are the subject of the experiment.

Timescales and delay methods

An optical delay line covers femtoseconds to a few nanoseconds. Electronic delay extends TA to nanoseconds, microseconds and beyond, which is needed for triplets and charge-separated states.

Pitfalls worth checking

IssueWhat to do
Pump fluenceStay in the linear regime; avoid multi-exciton effects.
PhotodamageStir or flow the sample and re-check its absorption after the measurement.
Coherent artifactSignals near Δt = 0 from pump–probe overlap; do not fit them.
White-light chirpDifferent wavelengths reach t₀ at different times; correct before fitting.
Solvent and cuvetteMeasure the solvent and cuvette alone.
PolarizationUse the magic angle between pump and probe for population kinetics.

Summary

  • The pump creates excited states at Δt = 0.
  • The probe, delayed by Δt, measures the change in absorption.
  • Negative ΔA: ground-state bleach and stimulated emission.
  • Positive ΔA: excited-state and product absorption.

X-ray luminescence & imaging

X-ray-excited luminescence, imaging and detection

Principle

An incident X-ray deposits energy in a scintillator, generating excited carriers that transfer energy to emitting states. The resulting visible or near-visible light is read by a photodetector, camera, or spectrometer. For imaging, variations in X-ray transmission through an object become a spatial light pattern in the scintillation screen.

OutcomeScintillation spectra, dose-dependent response, and image detail. Calibrated dose and background measurements support sensitivity estimates; spatial-resolution tests and screen thickness help explain imaging performance.

Principle reference: Hamamatsu · Indirect X-ray imaging
09

XPS

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

Principle

Monochromatic X-rays strike the sample and eject electrons by the photoelectric effect. An electron-energy analyzer measures their kinetic energies; the known photon energy and the instrument work-function correction give the binding energies. Because only electrons escaping from near the surface are detected efficiently, XPS primarily probes the outermost nanometres.

OutcomeSurface elements, relative atomic composition, and chemical-state shifts. Peak fitting, background choice, and charge referencing matter; small shifts alone should not be assigned to a chemical change without supporting evidence.

Principle reference: Kratos · XPS
10

SEM / EDS

Scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

Principle

A focused electron beam scans the specimen point by point. Secondary electrons give strong surface-detail contrast, while backscattered electrons can provide contrast related to composition and geometry. An EDS detector separately measures characteristic X-rays generated by electron-induced ionization, allowing elemental spectra and maps to be collected.

OutcomeParticle morphology, layered structures, and elemental distribution. EDS samples a finite interaction volume and has overlapping lines and detection limits, so a map is not direct atomic-scale proof of uniform composition.

Principle reference: JEOL · SEM and EDS
11

TEM / HRTEM

Transmission and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy

Principle

An electron-transparent specimen is illuminated by a high-energy electron beam. Transmitted and scattered electrons are focused by electromagnetic lenses to form an image or diffraction pattern. In HRTEM, interference between transmitted and diffracted waves can produce lattice contrast; the appearance depends on thickness, orientation, and imaging conditions.

OutcomeNanocrystal size and shape, interfaces, and lattice periodicity. Lattice fringes and diffraction help assess crystallinity, while representative sampling and beam-damage checks are essential for sensitive perovskites.

Principle reference: JEOL · High-resolution TEM
12

ICP-MS

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry

Principle

A liquid sample is nebulized into fine droplets and carried into a hot argon plasma, where it is desolvated, atomized, and ionized. Extracted ions pass into a mass spectrometer that separates them by mass-to-charge ratio before detection. Blanks, internal standards, and calibration solutions connect the measured ion counts to concentration.

OutcomeTrace elemental concentrations and, where the method supports it, isotope ratios. Digestion recovery, contamination, matrix effects, and spectral interferences must be controlled for a reliable bulk composition.

Principle reference: Agilent · ICP-MS
13

ICP-OES

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy

Principle

A nebulizer introduces a sample aerosol into an argon plasma, which atomizes and excites the elements. As excited atoms and ions relax, they emit light at element-specific wavelengths. A spectrometer resolves the emission lines, and calibrated line intensities provide the elemental concentrations.

OutcomeMulti-element bulk composition over an appropriate working range. Suitable digestion, blank correction, line selection, and matrix-matched calibration help separate true composition from spectral and sample-introduction effects.

Principle reference: Agilent · ICP-OES
14

AFM

Atomic force microscopy

Principle

A sharp tip mounted on a flexible cantilever moves across the surface. Tip–sample interactions change the cantilever deflection or oscillation, commonly monitored by a laser reflected onto a photodetector. A feedback loop adjusts the vertical scanner to maintain the selected interaction signal, building a height map as the tip scans.

OutcomeSurface topography, roughness, and apparent flake or film-step height. Tip shape broadens lateral features, and adsorbates or the substrate can affect measured heights, so layer-number assignments need supporting evidence.

Principle reference: Bruker · AFM principles
15

PLQY

Photoluminescence quantum yield

Principle

Photoluminescence quantum yield is the number of emitted photons divided by the number of absorbed excitation photons. In an absolute measurement, an integrating sphere collects light over many directions and the sample is compared with an appropriate reference or blank. Detector-response correction and the loss of excitation light are used to determine the photon balance.

OutcomeHow efficiently absorbed light is converted into luminescence. Reabsorption, scattering, and small absorption differences can bias the result; quantum yield complements emission spectra and lifetime measurements.

Principle reference: HORIBA · Quantum yield and lifetime

Profilometry

Surface and thin-film profilometry

Principle

A stylus traverses a surface while its vertical motion is recorded. Crossing a well-defined exposed-substrate/film step provides a direct height difference after leveling the profile. The scan can also describe roughness and waviness, with the result depending on stylus radius, applied force, and filtering.

OutcomeFilm thickness from a valid step and surface-height variations. A clean reference step and repeat scans are important; a rough surface trace alone does not establish film thickness.

Principle reference: Bruker · Stylus profilometry
17

TGA / DTA / DSC

Thermal analysis

Principle

A sample follows a controlled temperature program under a selected atmosphere. TGA records mass changes, DTA records the temperature difference between sample and reference, and DSC measures a heat-flow difference. These complementary signals distinguish events such as solvent loss, decomposition, glass transitions, melting, and crystallization when interpreted together.

OutcomeThermal stability, mass-loss steps, and thermal transitions. Heating rate and atmosphere affect the result; a thermal peak should be assigned using supporting chemistry rather than its shape alone.

Principle reference: NETZSCH · Thermal analysis
18

Dielectric & electrical

Electrical transport and dielectric characterization

Principle

Electrodes apply a known voltage or current to a sample, and the complementary electrical response is measured. DC measurements probe steady transport, while AC impedance tracks the magnitude and phase of the response across frequency. Electrode area and sample thickness convert the measured quantities into conductivity, permittivity, or dielectric loss using an appropriate model.

OutcomeTransport and polarization behavior, including frequency-dependent relaxation. Contacts, leakage, and electrode polarization can dominate the signal and should be evaluated before assigning a bulk-material mechanism.

Principle reference: Keysight · Dielectric measurements
19

Magnetic measurements

Magnetic characterization

Principle

Magnetic characterization measures how a specimen responds to an applied field or changing temperature. In the vibrating-sample method illustrated, the specimen oscillates between pickup coils and its magnetic moment induces an alternating voltage. Calibration and subtraction of holder and background signals convert that voltage into a sample magnetic moment.

OutcomeField-dependent magnetization, hysteresis, and temperature-dependent response. Normalization by sample mass and checks for contamination are important, especially when the material’s intrinsic signal is weak.

Principle reference: Quantum Design · Magnetometry
